\paragraph{Failure cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:failures} lists four Tier~2 errors; their anomaly maps are in the repository
(\texttt{results/stage8\_analysis}).
In \#1 the melt lies among dense components and the map stays low there; its maximum is in the
background, so the defect is simply not seen.
In \#2 the score is high, but it comes from the object--background boundary, not from the tiny spot,
so the near miss is coincidental.
In \#3 and \#4 the board sits slightly off its usual position, and the maximum falls on the background
or the board edge.
The shared blind spot is position: a location-wise Gaussian treats a shifted object as anomalous at its
edges, and this edge response masks small defects.
Over all test anomalies, the Tier~2 map peaks inside the defect mask (dilated by 8\,px) for 25.3\% of
images (0\% for capsules), against 70.3\% for Tier~3.
The first fix we would try is registering each image to a reference pose before scoring, or a
position-free patch score as in Tier~3.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
H1 is only partly supported.
Capsules has the largest Tier~3 $-$ Tier~2 gap ($+0.196$ PR-AUC), but on macaroni2 Tier~2 is better
(0.544 vs.\ 0.492, both near chance), and the multiple-instance group gap ($+0.095$) equals the
complex-structure gap.
H2 holds at every defect size: patch-level scoring recalls more anomalies than global pooling for small
($<$0.25\%: 0.770 vs.\ 0.742), medium (0.834 vs.\ 0.800) and large defects ($>$0.98\%: 0.983 vs.\ 0.969).

\paragraph{Practical impact.}
At a 1\% defect rate, 10,000 inspected items contain 100 defects.
With the best-F2 threshold, Tier~2 misses 18 of them and sends 3,288 good items to re-inspection, so
only 2.4\% of flagged items are defective; Tier~3 misses about the same number (18.5) but raises 1,722
false alarms, 1,565 fewer, which roughly halves the inspectors' workload.
If the line can re-inspect only about 2\% of production, Tier~2 needs the 99th-percentile threshold
(186 false alarms) and then misses 71 of 100 defects.
Choosing between inspection labour and escaped defects is therefore the quality manager's decision, and
Tier~3 moves the whole trade-off in their favour.
These figures use the class-averaged TPR and FPR, so they differ slightly from prec$_{1\%}$ in
Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\paragraph{Interpretability.}
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps} explains why one anomaly was flagged.
Tier~2 flags the chewing gum with a large margin ($s/\tau = 5.19$), but its map peaks on the shadow
above the object, not on the missing chunk; Tier~3 peaks on the defect itself.
For a missing component on pcb4 (in the repository), both maps peak on the empty pad, so an engineer
could act on either.
Mean pixel ROC-AUC is 0.925 for Tier~2 and 0.973 for Tier~3.
We would show engineers the Tier~3 map as a localisation aid and not trust the Tier~2 map, which points
at the defect in only a quarter of cases.
On cashew, pcb1 and pcb2 a high score may also reflect the acquisition shortcut of \S\ref{sec:eda}
rather than the defect, which is why these objects are reported separately.
