\section{Exploratory Feature Analysis}
\label{sec:eda}

All statistics in this section use train normals and val images only; test images are never read.
Each image is represented by the spatially averaged layer2 and layer3 activations of an
ImageNet-pretrained \citep{deng2009imagenet} WideResNet-50 \citep{zagoruyko2016wide}, giving 1,536
dimensions.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{stage4_eda/fig_eda_paper.png}
  \caption{Normal vs.\ anomalous images in the global embedding, for one object per pattern.
    Top: PCA fitted on train normals (axes are unitless projections). Bottom: Mahalanobis distance to the
    train-normal Gaussian, val normal vs.\ val anomaly; KS is the two-sample statistic. All 12 objects are
    plotted in the repository (\texttt{results/stage4\_eda}).}
  \label{fig:eda}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Distribution contrast.}
In the PCA projection (Figure~\ref{fig:eda}, top), val anomalies fall inside the train-normal cloud for
most objects and form no cluster of their own, so a single global embedding is a weak detector.
We fit a Ledoit--Wolf Gaussian \citep{ledoit2004well} to the train normals and compare the Mahalanobis
distances of val normals and val anomalies (bottom).
The two-sample KS statistic is between 0.66 and 0.91 for ten objects.
It is only 0.45 for macaroni2 and 0.30 for capsules, where the difference is not significant
($p = 0.12$).
Both weak objects contain several freely placed instances.

\paragraph{Dispersion and redundancy.}
The per-location variance of layer3 features across train normals is 10.4 for capsules and 7.8 for
macaroni2, against 1.3--3.6 for the other ten objects.
Objects whose instances move in the frame are therefore much less regular at a fixed position, which is
exactly what a location-wise Gaussian assumes.
The candidate features are also redundant.
Layer2 and layer3 have a linear CKA \citep{kornblith2019similarity} of 0.68--0.97, and 109--287 of the
1,536 pooled dimensions explain 95\% of the variance.
This redundancy is why Tier~2 keeps a random subset of channels instead of all of them
(\S\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Acquisition shortcut.}
For three objects the val anomalies concentrate in the smaller of two $k$-means modes of the train
normals: cashew (35.8\% of normals vs.\ 95.0\% of anomalies), pcb1 (20.1\% vs.\ 95.0\%) and pcb2
(23.2\% vs.\ 70.0\%).
Figure~\ref{fig:eda} shows this for pcb1, where almost all anomalies sit in the minor PCA cluster.
Simple image statistics explain the pattern.
Anomalous cashew images are brighter than normals (mean grey level 62.7 vs.\ 53.1 and 55.8 in the two
modes), and so are anomalous pcb2 images (97.7 vs.\ 86.8 and 91.0).
For pcb1, 45\% of anomalous images are rotated by 180\textdegree, against about 7\% of normals.
A detector can therefore score well on these objects without seeing the defect.
We flagged every object with a gap of at least 40 percentage points before computing any test result,
and we report each main result on the remaining nine objects as well.

\paragraph{Hypotheses.}
The evidence above leads to three hypotheses, which we revisit in \S\ref{sec:results} and
\S\ref{sec:analysis}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1:} a location-wise Gaussian (Tier~2) loses most to a location-free
    nearest-neighbour model (Tier~3) on objects with high positional variance (capsules, macaroni2).
    Tested by the per-object Tier~3 $-$ Tier~2 gap.
  \item \textbf{H2:} small defects are diluted in global features, so patch-level scoring is needed.
    Tested by the feature ablation (patch-level vs.\ global pooled) and by detection rate per defect
    size.
  \item \textbf{H3:} aggregating the anomaly map by its maximum is more sensitive to small defects than
    a top-1\% mean. Tested by the aggregation ablation.
\end{itemize}
